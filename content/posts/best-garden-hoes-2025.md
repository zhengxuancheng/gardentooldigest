I spent years weeding on my hands and knees with a hand fork, which is a great way to get intimate with your soil and a terrible way to spend an afternoon. A hoe changes the math entirely. Stand up, swing, and the weeds are gone at the root, row after row, without bending over once. For clearing a vegetable bed or maintaining a flower border, nothing else comes close.

I tested six hoes across a full growing season: heavy draw hoes for breaking ground, stirrup hoes for slicing weeds, a Dutch hoe for standing work, and a hori hori for the tight spots. I used them in clay, in sandy loam, and in a gravel path that ate one cheap blade. Here's what I learned.

## What to Look For in a Garden Hoe

### Draw, Stirrup, or Dutch

The three main hoe styles do different jobs. A draw hoe has a blade set at a right angle to the handle; you chop down and pull, which makes it the tool for breaking new ground and hilling. A stirrup hoe (also called a scuffle or action hoe) has a loop of steel that you push and pull just under the soil surface, slicing weeds off at the root. It's the fastest weeding tool ever made. A Dutch hoe has a flat blade you push through the soil in front of you, great for standing work in established beds. Buy based on the job: breaking soil says draw hoe, maintaining beds says stirrup or Dutch.

### Blade Steel and Sharpness

The blade is the tool. Cheap hoes are stamped from thin steel that bends on the first rock and dulls within a season. Quality hoes use 10 to 12 gauge steel or forged heads that hold an edge. Some brands machine-sharpen the leading edge from the factory, which matters because a sharp hoe cuts weeds, while a dull one just pushes them over. A hoe you can't sharpen is a disposable tool wearing a garden's clothes.

### Handle Length

Handle length decides your posture. A 54 to 60 inch handle lets you work standing straight, which is the entire point of a hoe. Shorter handles force you to hunch, and a hunched weeder quits early. For most adults, aim for a handle that reaches your waist or higher when the head is on the ground. If you're tall, look for 60 inch options or extension handles.

### Weight and Balance

A heavy hoe breaks ground better; a light hoe is easier to swing all afternoon. The sweet spot for most people is a head around 1.5 to 2 pounds on a balanced handle. A well-balanced hoe should feel neutral in your hands, with the head weight doing the work instead of your arms. Test the balance by holding it in the middle: if it tips hard toward the head, expect a sore back.

### Durability and Warranty

A good hoe is a decades-long purchase. Look for solid construction: a head that's forged or welded to the handle ferrule, not glued. Fiberglass handles outlast wood, but wood feels better in the hand. Several of the brands below back their tools with lifetime or multi-year warranties, which is a strong signal about how long they expect the tool to last.

## FAQ

### What kind of hoe is best for weeding?

A stirrup hoe, also called an action or scuffle hoe. The steel loop slices weeds off just below the surface on both the push and the pull, which makes it the fastest weeding tool for vegetable beds and borders. For deep-rooted or taproot weeds, follow up with a hori hori or a hand fork.

### How do I keep a garden hoe sharp?

Use a flat file or a sharpening stone along the leading edge, a few passes every couple of weeks during heavy use. A sharp hoe cuts weeds; a dull one pushes them over and makes you swing harder. Stamped blades dull faster than forged or boron steel, which is one reason premium hoes cost more.

### Draw hoe or stirrup hoe: which should I get?

If you're breaking new ground, hilling, or working heavy soil, get a draw hoe. If you're maintaining existing beds and weeding regularly, get a stirrup hoe. Most gardens end up needing both. If you can only afford one, the stirrup hoe does more of the weekly work, and a draw hoe can be rented or borrowed for the occasional ground-breaking job.

### How long should the handle be?

Long enough that you can work standing straight, which for most adults means 54 to 60 inches. If the handle is too short, you'll hunch and quit early. Tall gardeners should specifically look for 60-inch handles like the Truper or the Hoss. Wood handles are worth the care they need, because they absorb shock better than fiberglass.

### What's a hori hori used for?

A hori hori is a Japanese gardening knife with a sharp edge and a serrated edge on one blade. It weeds, digs, transplants, cuts roots, and measures planting depth, all without bending over for a different tool. It's the best companion to a hoe for the tight spots and stubborn taproots a full-size tool can't reach.
